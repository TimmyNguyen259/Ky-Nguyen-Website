A flowchart tells you what happens. It rarely tells you who owns the call when the flowchart is wrong. Most processes are written for the case that goes to plan and quietly depend on someone improvising the rest — which is how a clean process can still be a bad system.

The gap sits at the handoffs. An arrow between two boxes says the work moves. It does not say who owns the work while it moves, or who decides when it arrives incomplete. Those are the moments a process gets tested, and they are the ones it leaves blank.

So the call goes to whoever answers fastest, or whoever is most senior in the thread. It works, which is the problem. Each exception resolved that way teaches the people closest to the work that the real system is knowing whom to ask. Over time that habit becomes the system — undocumented, and dependent on the same few people being available. When it breaks, the instinct is to blame whoever was standing at the handoff. Usually the system never told them the call was theirs.

The tools for closing that gap are not new, and none of them closes it alone. I use SOPs, swimlanes and RACI, and each answers a different question:

**An SOP says what the steps are.** It is necessary, and it is rarely where a process fails.

**A swimlane shows where the work crosses between roles.** Every lane change is a handoff, and every handoff needs a clear point where ownership passes.

**A RACI names who is accountable for the call.** One role, not a committee. It is also the easiest of the three to fill in politely, with accountability spread so no one is exposed. If two roles are marked accountable, no one is.

A plain test follows. Take the most common ways a process breaks and ask who decides each one. If the answer is a role, there is a system. If the answer is a name, there is a person holding the system together, and the design work has not been done yet. If the same exception keeps returning to the same person, it belongs in the design — as a rule, a threshold or a changed step — not in another escalation.

This is the core of the operating-model work I am doing now: building the foundation of a shared service center inside an SSC · HRBP · COE model. The steps are the easy part to draw. The harder part is deciding work scope, role ownership and where each handoff sits across the employee lifecycle. I am preparing a phased transfer of selected people processes, with recruitment as the pilot case. That work is still in progress, and I do not yet know which of those handoffs will hold once real volume runs through them. That is what a pilot is for.

Redesigning a process without redesigning who decides produces a faster version of the same escalation. Decision rights are the part of a system people feel when something goes wrong, so they are the part worth writing down first.

> Decide who owns the call. Then draw the flowchart.
